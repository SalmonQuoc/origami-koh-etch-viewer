    How this scene etches a shape

    Each wall is a crystal plane that moves out from the opening at its own rate (n·x ≤ h + R·t). A non-convex shape (L, U, cross, frame, ring, star) is split into overlapping convex pieces; each piece etches as a convex opening and the cavity shown is their union. Concave corners therefore stop at {111} as they should. A drawn outline is split into convex pieces by triangulation (a design choice); where two pieces meet along a ⟨110⟩ line, a thin ridge can stay between their cavities. Convex mask corners (the inside corner of an L or U, the tips of a star) are not undercut here, although they are in real KOH etching (Kovacs et al. 1998, Fig. 1(c); Pal et al. 2007, Sec. 2). Openings that grow into each other are overlaid, not merged. The etch simulator handles both.

    Model, sources and assumptions
    • Rates (sourced): KOH 30, 40, 50 wt% at 70 °C for 12 orientations, Pal & Sato Table 4.1, p. 87. KOH 42 wt% at 75 °C: {100} 42, {110} 66, {111} 0.5 µm/h, Lec 4-1 p. 7; its other 9 orientations are the Pal & Sato 40 wt% values scaled by R(100) (assumption).
    • Rates between the measured orientations (assumption): linear interpolation over a triangulation of the 12 orientations in the standard triangle (gnomonic coordinates), with cubic symmetry. It keeps the cusp at {111}: rates grow linearly with the off-angle, as measured for vicinal (111) (Pal & Sato p. 104).
    • Corner rules (sourced, not imposed): concave corners show the slowest plane and convex corners the fastest (Lec 4-1 p. 8). The simulator does not apply these rules; they come out of the rate map.
    • Front motion (method): every surface element moves along its normal at R(hkl); this is solved as a 3D level set (Lax-Friedrichs scheme with second-order differences and Heun time steps, fast-marching re-initialisation) on a cubic grid. Accuracy is about one cell; sharp edges are rounded over about two cells. Check: 200 µm square window on (100), 60 min in 30 wt%: depth 47.32 µm (4 µm grid) and 47.56 µm (2 µm grid) against 0.797 × 60 = 47.82 µm; {111} wall 54.73° (4 µm grid) and 54.69° (2 µm grid) against 54.74°.
    • Mask edge (assumption): no etchant reaches silicon through the mask, so silicon under the mask is only removed from the side. At the top row under the mask the level set has no downward slope; where the wall there is near-vertical (slope below 0.35), the vertical gradient is set to zero. Check: a 100 µm square window at 45° on (100), 40 min in 30 wt% KOH, gives 26.5 µm sideways undercut at a 2 µm grid (29.3 µm at 1 µm) against 0.797 × 40 = 31.9 µm (Pal & Sato Table 4.1, p. 87); a ⟨110⟩-aligned pit keeps 0 µm. Use a fine grid when {100} undercut matters.
    • Known numerical limits: silicon walls and beams narrower than about 8 grid cells thin slowly (numerical dissipation, not physics), most when they are oblique to the grid; windows narrower than about 5 cells etch too slowly. Use the “fine” or “very fine” resolution or wider features when this matters. Undercut values are good to about one to two cells. The sideways undercut of vertical {100} walls starts a few minutes late and then keeps a constant lag of about two cells (4.0 µm at a 2 µm grid, 2.3 µm at 1 µm, the same after 20, 40 and 60 min), so its relative error falls for longer etches.
    • Convex corners (check, known limit): a (100) mesa corner is bevelled at 24° in 34 wt% KOH here, against 30 ± 1° measured (Pal & Sato p. 231; Mayer et al. 1990, p. 3948: 30.96°). The simulator agrees within 1.5° with the bevel its own rate map predicts (minimum envelope, Pal & Sato p. 228); the difference comes from the 12-orientation rate map, which has no {411} data (How it works, part 6).
    • Shallow {111} walls on (110) (check): a groove with ⟨110⟩ edges on (110) has {111} side walls at 35.26° to the surface (Pal & Sato p. 213, Fig. 6.2); the simulator gives 33.9° (4 µm grid) and 34.7° (2 µm grid) after 30 min in 30 wt% KOH, with the floor at 43.61 µm against 1.455 × 30 = 43.65 µm.
    • Mask (assumption): inert for the etch; etchant reaches the silicon only through openings and under released mask. The film is drawn at its true thickness (default 1 µm thermal SiO₂, a design choice inside the 1–2 µm of Pal & Sato p. 348; or 0.15 µm LPCVD Si₃N₄, Pal & Sato p. 54) and a SiO₂ film thins at the oxide rate below; when it is used up the readout says so, but the model still keeps the mask. Pal & Sato p. 83: a 2–3 µm thermal oxide is not enough to etch through a 500 µm wafer in KOH (Si : SiO₂ selectivity about 150); Si₃N₄ is used instead. For KOH etchants with no oxide rate in the course files the rate is estimated as R(100)/150 (estimate). The SiO₂ loss shown uses 0.34 µm/h for KOH 42 wt% (thermal oxide, Lec 4-1 p. 7; Lec 10 p. 11) and the Lec 10 p. 11 values for KOH 57 wt% and EDP Type S; for the other KOH etchants it is the R(100)/150 estimate above, and for EDP F&K it is not in the course files.
    • Rates from Lec 10 and EDP: KOH 57 wt% at 75 °C: {100} 25, {110} 39, {111} 0.5 µm/h (Lec 10 p. 11); its other orientations are the Pal & Sato 50 wt% values scaled by R(100) (assumption). EDP Type S at 110 °C: {100} 51, {110} 57, {111} 1.25 µm/h (Lec 10 p. 11). EDP F&K: ratio 50:30:3 (Finne and Klein 1967, Berkeley lab manual ch. 1.10) scaled to R(100) = 51 µm/h (assumption). For EDP only three orientations are given, so all other planes use the three-rate interpolation of Radjenović et al. (2010, eq. 1) (assumption). Lec 10 p. 16 orders EDP as (100) > (110) > (111), which the Type S values of p. 11 do not follow; F&K does.
    • Wafer: 525 µm thick by default (100 mm wafer, Pal & Sato Table 2.1, p. 30); only the depth that the etch can reach is simulated, and the back side is protected (assumption). Frames: (100) x = [1̄10] along the flat (origami tutorial, Rev. 1); (110) two conventions, see above; (111) flat along [11̄0] (assumption).
    • Pre-etch (assumption): a vertical prism of the opening shape, as by deep RIE (Lec 4-1 p. 16), before KOH.
    • Compensation data (sourced): ⟨100⟩ beam width W = 2d (Pal & Sato p. 249); ⟨110⟩ beam d = (0.5 W cos α + L sin α)/Ur (Pal & Sato p. 244); KOH turns a 90° convex corner into 150 ± 2° (Pal & Sato p. 235). Example dimensions not covered by these are design choices.

    Find the true crystal orientation

    The flat of a real wafer is only good to 2–5° (Lec 10 p. 18; 1° to 5°, Pal & Sato p. 294), so mask edges drawn along the flat miss ⟨110⟩ by a flat error δ. Here the crystal is turned by δ. Put test structures on the mask, etch them, read them under the microscope, and turn the device mask by the estimate before the device etch (Lec 10 pp. 18, 25–26; Pal & Sato Ch. 7).

    Method, sources and assumptions
    • Flat error (sourced): ⟨110⟩ flat 2–5° (Lec 10 pp. 18, 25); 1° to 5° (Pal & Sato p. 294). Pre-etched patterns reach 0.01° (Pal & Sato p. 296). Misalignment widens a channel of length l by U = l sin δ cos δ (Pal & Sato eq. 7.1, p. 289: 18 µm for 10 mm at 0.1°).
    • Slot fan (method after Lec 10 p. 18, “long slot gives around 1°”): long slots at stepped angles. In the long-etch limit a slot of length L and width w misaligned by α etches to the ⟨110⟩ rectangle of width w cos α + L sin α (3 cm at 1° gives 524 µm, Lec 10 p. 18). The narrowest slot gives δ to ± half a step; the widths of its two neighbours give a V-shaped fit. Fan size, step and spacing are design choices.
    • Circle arc (sourced): circles on an arc of radius R at pitch p, so neighbours turn by p/R. On (100) each circle etches to an inverted pyramid with ⟨110⟩ edges; the pair whose line of centres is parallel to ⟨110⟩ has aligned edges (Ensell, in Pal & Sato pp. 297–299: 41 circles Ø 75 µm, 78.5 µm apart, 0.1°, R = 45 mm; Lec 10 p. 18: 80 µm pitch, tan⁻¹(80/45000) = 0.1°). On (110) the circles etch to hexagons and the bow-tie corners of neighbours line up (Tseng and Chang, Lec 10 pp. 25–26: φ/360° = (√6 r + x)/(2πR), R = 48.9 mm, 0.06°). Here the edge offset between neighbours k and k+1 is read as the shift of their edges along the true edge normal, p sin(τ − δ).
    • Inconsistency in the source: Lec 10 p. 25 gives 0.06° with a 35 µm pitch and R = 48.9 mm, but 35/48 900 rad = 0.041°. The lecture layout here uses r = 20 µm and x = 2 µm, so p = √6 r + x = 51 µm and φ = 0.060° at R = 48.9 mm (design choice that matches the stated 0.06°).
    • Etching (method): each structure is etched with the analytic plane model of the Etched pit (walls n·x ≤ h + R t; long-etch limit: {111} only), with the crystal turned by δ. Structures do not interact (cavities that grow into each other are overlaid, not merged).
    • Reading (assumption): every length is rounded to the readable length. Real limits not modelled: mask-writer angle steps (Chang and Huang, in Pal & Sato p. 304), edge roughness, rate scatter, θ error of the second alignment.
    • Random wafers: δ uniform in ±5° (upper end of Lec 10 p. 18); readings from the long-etch formulas above, which the analytic etch reproduces (checked).
    Model on wafer and Etched pit: each wall is a crystal plane that moves outward at its own rate from the mask opening (n·x ≤ h + R·t over the {100}, {110} and {111} planes); the long-etch limit keeps only {111} planes with R = 0. This is the construction used in the origami tutorial; non-convex openings are split there into overlapping convex pieces whose cavities are joined, and convex mask corners are not undercut; use the etch simulator for that (its model and sources are listed in its panel). Rates: Pal & Sato, p. 87 (KOH 30 wt% and 40 wt%, 70 °C); Lec 4-1, p. 7 (KOH 42 wt%, 75 °C); Lec 10, p. 11 (KOH 57 wt%, 75 °C; EDP Type S, 110 °C); EDP 50:30:3 ratio after Finne and Klein (Berkeley lab manual ch. 1.10).     How it works: chemistry, physics and the model

    Study notes behind the page. Each part gives the idea, the equation, a number you can check on the page and where to see it. Click a title to open it. Sources are given in parentheses; (assumption) and (method of this page) mark what is not from a source.

    1. What KOH does to silicon

    KOH, EDP, TMAH, NaOH and hydrazine solutions all etch silicon by the same overall reaction (Pal & Sato p. 79, eq. 4.1):

    Si + 2 H₂O + 2 OH⁻ → Si(OH)₂O₂²⁻ + 2 H₂ (gas)

    Silicon atoms are hydroxylated (OH groups attach to them) one bond at a time, until they leave as a water-soluble ion; hydrogen bubbles form as a by-product. The reaction needs both OH⁻ and water (Pal & Sato p. 79). Step by step (eqs. 4.2–4.5, pp. 80–82): a hydrogen-terminated surface atom gets an OH⁻ in place of an H, then a second one; the hydroxylated atom then breaks its back bonds with water and leaves as Si(OH)₄, which OH⁻ turns into the soluble Si(OH)₂O₂²⁻. The cation (K⁺) takes no part in these steps, which is why many alkaline solutions show a similar anisotropy (Pal & Sato p. 82). Alkaline etchants with pH above 12 etch anisotropically; the acid mixture HNA etches isotropically (Hsu p. 326; Lec 4-1 p. 2).

    2. Why the rate depends on the crystal plane

    Silicon has the diamond lattice: every atom has four bonds (Pal & Sato p. 79). On a cut surface some bonds are left open (“dangling bonds”), and these are where the reaction starts (pp. 80–81):

    • (100): two dangling bonds per surface atom, so it is attacked readily.
    • (110): one dangling bond, but the back bonds lie parallel to the surface and are exposed, so they are hydroxylated easily.
    • (111): one dangling bond and all three back bonds go to atoms that have no dangling bonds, so the surface atom is stable. This is why (111) is hardly etched.

    Check it: in 30 wt% KOH at 70 °C, R(100) = 0.797 µm/min and R(111) = 0.005 µm/min (Pal & Sato Table 4.1, p. 87), a ratio of about 160. Hsu (p. 325) quotes up to 400 : 1 for ⟨100⟩ to ⟨111⟩. The fastest orientation is not always (110): at 30 wt% (210), (320) and (530) are faster than (110) in Table 4.1, and in the measured map for 40 % KOH the maximum lies away from (110) (p. 85). See it: colour the simulator by “etch rate of the wall”.

    3. Temperature: the Arrhenius law

    KOH etching is reaction limited: the slow step is the attachment of OH⁻ to surface atoms, which needs thermal energy (Pal & Sato p. 90). The rate therefore follows the Arrhenius law (p. 90):

    R = R₀ exp(−Q / kT)

    with T the absolute temperature, Q the activation energy and k Boltzmann’s constant (8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K, standard constant, not in the course files). Q(100) ≈ 0.60 eV in 40 % KOH, each orientation has a slightly different Q, and {111} has the smallest, so the anisotropy changes with temperature (p. 90). The custom-etchant dialog uses the ratio form, which removes the unknown R₀:

    R(T) = R(T₀) · exp[ −(Q/k) · (1/T − 1/T₀) ]

    Other measurements. Seidel et al. (1990) measured Q(100) = 0.57, 0.59 and 0.61 eV in 20, 42 and 57 % KOH (Fig. 12, p. 3618) and Q(110) = 0.59 eV against Q(100) = 0.57 eV in 20 % KOH (Fig. 17, p. 3620), so in KOH (100) and (110) change almost alike. In EDP Type S the spread is larger: 0.33 eV for (110), 0.40 eV for (100) and 0.52 eV for (111) (Fig. 13, p. 3619). The sources disagree on (111): Pal & Sato (p. 90) find the smallest Q for (111) in 40 % KOH, Seidel et al. (abstract, p. 3612) find that slowly etching planes have the higher Q. The page uses these values as defaults in the custom-etchant dialog and 0.60 eV for (111) in KOH (assumption). Pal et al. (2021, section “Etching at boiling point of the etchant”) report that the Arrhenius plots of (100) and (110) stay nearly straight from 80 °C up to near the boiling point.

    Check it: with Q = 0.60 eV, going from 40 °C to 80 °C multiplies the rate by 12.4 (10.9 with Seidel’s 0.57 eV); Pal & Sato (p. 90) report about 10 times for 40 % KOH. Moving 30 wt% KOH from 70 °C to 80 °C multiplies by 1.78, so R(100) goes from 0.797 to 1.42 µm/min. Lec 4-1 p. 7 summarises the trend: temperature up, rate up, selectivity down, roughness up.

    4. Concentration

    Above about 25 wt% KOH the rate falls as the concentration rises (Pal & Sato p. 88). The reason is in the reaction: water is a reactant and KOH acts as a catalyst, so a more concentrated solution has less water to react (p. 88). Not all planes change alike: (100) and (221) stay almost constant from 17 to 51 wt%, while (110), (210) and (320) change more (p. 88), so the anisotropy, and with it the etched shape, depends on the concentration (p. 89). Lec 4-1 p. 7: concentration up, rate down, selectivity up, roughness down.

    Check it: Table 4.1 (p. 87) gives R(100) = 0.797, 0.599 and 0.539 µm/min at 30, 40 and 50 wt%. The custom-etchant dialog interpolates linearly between these columns (assumption); at 35 wt% it gives R(100) = 0.698 µm/min. Seidel et al. (1990, Fig. 16, p. 3620) found that above about 20 % KOH the (100) rate follows R ∝ [H₂O]⁴[KOH]^¼, the water dependence named in Pal & Sato p. 88.

    Where the sources do not agree. (1) The text of p. 88 says (100) stays almost constant from 17 to 51 wt%, but Table 4.1 (p. 87) has R(100) fall from 0.797 to 0.539 µm/min, by 32 %, from 30 to 50 wt%. (2) Table 4.1 interpolated to 42 wt% and moved from 70 °C to 75 °C with Q = 0.60 eV gives {100} 47, {110} 97 and {111} 0.72 µm/h, while Lec 4-1 p. 7 gives 42, 66 and 0.5 µm/h for KOH 42 wt% at 75 °C: {100} agrees within 12 %, {110} and {111} are about 1.5 times lower in the lecture. Different solutions, stirring and wafers give different rates, so the page keeps each source’s own numbers (the two etchants “KOH 40 wt%, 70 °C” and “KOH 42 wt%, 75 °C”) rather than mixing them.

    5. The mask and selectivity

    The selectivity of a mask is the etch rate of silicon divided by the etch rate of the mask material in the same etchant; the higher it is, the better the mask (Hsu p. 327). KOH dissolves thermal SiO₂ noticeably: the Si to SiO₂ selectivity is about 150 under normal conditions, so 2–3 µm of oxide is not enough to etch through a 500 µm wafer, and Si₃N₄ is used instead (Pal & Sato p. 83; Hsu p. 327). Hot, concentrated KOH lowers it further: 40 at 110 °C and 25 at 135 °C in 45 wt% KOH (Pal & Sato p. 92).

    Check it: KOH 42 wt% etches (100) at 42 µm/h and thermal oxide at 0.34 µm/h (Lec 4-1 p. 7), a selectivity of 124. Through 500 µm takes 500/42 = 11.9 h and removes 4.0 µm of oxide, in line with Pal & Sato’s warning. See it: the Etched pit and the simulator draw the mask film at its true thickness (Show and Time sections: thermal SiO₂, 1 µm by default, or Si₃N₄). A SiO₂ film thins at the oxide rate of the etchant, and the “mask film” readout gives what is left or when it is used up. For KOH 30 wt% at 70 °C no oxide rate is in the course files, so the page estimates it as R(100)/150 = 0.797/150 µm/min = 0.32 µm/h: 1 µm lasts about 190 min. Try it: KOH 57 wt% (0.62 µm/h, Lec 10 p. 11) uses up 1 µm of oxide in 97 min, long before a 525 µm wafer is etched through.

    Selectivity and temperature. Oxide dissolves with a larger activation energy than silicon: Q(SiO₂) = 0.85 eV in 20 % and 0.86 eV in 42 % KOH (Seidel et al. 1990, Fig. 18, p. 3620) against about 0.60 eV for Si(100). The selectivity S = R(Si)/R(SiO₂) therefore falls with temperature as S(T) = S(T₀) · exp[(Q(SiO₂) − Q(Si))/k · (1/T − 1/T₀)]. Check it: from 110 °C to 135 °C this gives a factor exp[0.25 eV/k · (1/408 − 1/383) K⁻¹] = 0.629; Pal & Sato (p. 92) report 40 → 25 for 45 wt% KOH, a factor 0.625. Starting from the lecture’s 124 at 75 °C (42 wt%) it predicts 58 at 110 °C and 36 at 135 °C, against 40 and 25 in 45 wt% (p. 92): the same trend, about 1.4 times higher, in line with Seidel et al. (p. 3619), who find the ratio lower in more concentrated KOH. Seidel et al. (p. 3619) give 100–200 for concentrated KOH at usual temperatures and about 1000 for 20 % KOH at a lower temperature. See it: make a custom etchant from KOH 30 wt% at 90 °C: the dialog shows Si(100) : SiO₂ = 94 instead of 150, and the mask-film readout uses the faster oxide rate.

    6. The shapes that follow: 54.74°, V-grooves and corners

    Because {111} is almost not etched, a pit in a (100) wafer ends up bounded by {111} planes, which meet the surface at 54.74° (Hsu p. 325). The angle is arccos(1/√3) = 54.74°, and tan 54.74° = √2 (geometry). A square opening of side W with edges along ⟨110⟩ therefore stops at a depth of W/√2 = 0.707 W, when the four {111} walls meet in a point.

    Corners. At a concave corner the slowest plane shows and nothing is undercut; at a convex corner the fastest plane shows and the corner is cut back under the mask (Lec 4-1 p. 8; Lec 10 p. 14; Pal & Sato p. 180). Undercut is useful for releasing cantilevers and bridges, and a problem for mesas and proof masses, which need corner compensation (Pal & Sato p. 180; Lec 10 pp. 15–16).

    How a convex corner is cut back. The undercut front leaves the ⟨110⟩ mask edges along the direction of the largest lateral etch rate near them, at the bevel angle α to the edge (Pal & Sato pp. 226–236). Measured: α = 30 ± 1° in 35 wt% KOH at 76 °C (p. 231), so a 90° mesa corner becomes a 150 ± 2° corner (p. 235); Mayer et al. (1990, p. 3948) give 30.96° (the ⟨410⟩ trace of {411} planes) and a 151.9° corner (Fig. 2). The undercut ratio U = l/d (l along ⟨110⟩, d the depth) sets the size of a compensation structure (p. 237).

    Check it on this page: a 320 µm square mesa on (100) etched 40 min gives in the simulator α = 23.6°, 24.4°, 25.7° and 27.3° for 30, 34 (interpolated), 40 and 50 wt% KOH at 70 °C (2 µm grid; 24.2° at 1 µm and 4 µm, so it does not depend on the grid). The largest lateral rate of the same rate map, found by the minimum-envelope construction of p. 228, lies at 25.0°, 25.4°, 26.3° and 28.4°. The simulator therefore follows its rate map, and the gap of about 5° to the measured 30° comes from the rate map: Table 4.1 has no orientation between (100) and (311)/(310), so the {411} planes that bound the corner in KOH (Mayer et al. 1990) get interpolated rates (known limit). The undercut length l is not yet grid-converged (78, 97 and 109 µm at 4, 2 and 1 µm grids for 34 wt%), so compare corner sizes at one resolution only.

    See it: Etched pit with a 100 µm square: the final depth is 70.7 µm. Corner lab: compare an uncompensated mesa with a compensated one.

    7. A rate for every orientation (the rate map)

    Rates are measured for a few orientations only. Pal & Sato Table 4.1 (p. 87) lists 12, measured on hemispherical specimens that expose all orientations at once (p. 85); the lectures give {100}, {110} and {111} (Lec 4-1 p. 7; Lec 10 p. 11). The simulator needs R for any direction n, so the page fills in the gaps (method of this page):

    1. Cubic symmetry: take |n₁|, |n₂|, |n₃| and sort them so that a ≥ b ≥ c. Every direction then falls in one “standard triangle” whose corners are (100), (110) and (111).
    2. Map it to the plane u = b/a, v = c/a (gnomonic coordinates): (100) is (0, 0), (110) is (1, 0) and (111) is (1, 1).
    3. Triangulate the measured orientations in this plane (Delaunay) and interpolate linearly inside each triangle (assumption). With only three rates the whole triangle is one piece.

    Linear interpolation keeps the sharp minimum (cusp) at {111}: the rate grows linearly with the angle away from (111), as measured for vicinal (111) (Pal & Sato p. 104). The lecture KOH etchants (42 and 57 wt%) give three rates; their other nine planes are the Pal & Sato 40 or 50 wt% column scaled to the lecture R(100) (assumption). EDP has three rates only and uses the single triangle. The simulator panel “Model, sources and assumptions” lists each case.

    8. The Etched pit scene: moving planes

    Each plane family {100}, {110} and {111} contributes its planes with unit normals n. A plane starts where it just touches the opening, at h = max over the opening corners of n·x, and moves outward at its own rate R. The cavity after time t is the region inside all of them and below the surface (method of this page):

    n·x ≤ h + R t   for every plane n,   and z ≤ 0

    This is exact for a convex opening whose walls are flat planes. For long times only the slow {111} planes are left. Shapes that are not convex are split into convex pieces and the cavities are combined, so the scene cannot show convex-corner undercut; the simulator can.

    9. The etch simulator: the level-set method

    The simulator follows the silicon surface as the zero level of a function φ(x, y, z, t): φ > 0 in silicon, φ < 0 where silicon has been etched. Every point of the surface moves along its normal n = ∇φ/|∇φ| at the local rate R(n), which gives the level-set equation (standard numerical method; not in the course files):

    ∂φ/∂t + R(n) |∇φ| = 0

    How the page solves it (method of this page):

    • Grid. A cubic grid of cell size dx, set by the resolution: about 1.5 × 10⁵ (draft), 4 × 10⁵ (normal), 10⁶ (fine) or 2.5 × 10⁶ (very fine) cells over the simulated volume, with dx at least 0.5 µm. Run time grows faster than the number of cells, because the time step shrinks with dx. φ starts as the distance from each cell to the silicon exposed by the openings; the mask edge is found by an exact distance transform of the drawn mask, sampled four times finer than the grid.
    • Time step. Lax-Friedrichs: H = R(n)|p| with p the mean of the one-sided differences D⁺ᵢφ and D⁻ᵢφ, minus ½ Σ αᵢ (D⁺ᵢφ − D⁻ᵢφ). The one-sided differences are second order (ENO with the minmod limiter), and each step averages two forward-Euler stages (Heun). The αᵢ ≥ max |∂H/∂pᵢ| add the smoothing that keeps the scheme stable, and the step is dt = 0.9 dx / (α_x + α_y + α_z) (CFL condition).
    • Etching cannot be undone. φ is never allowed to rise, so a wall never grows back.
    • Narrow band. Only cells within 4 dx of the surface are updated; every 6 steps φ is reset to the distance from the surface by fast marching, which keeps |∇φ| close to 1. The cells just behind the front keep their values, so the reset does not move the front; moving it a little every few steps used to thin {111} walls, whose rate rises steeply away from {111}.
    • Mask. No etchant passes through the mask, so silicon under it is etched only from the side (assumption).
    • Arrival time. Each cell stores the time the front reached it, so the time slider and the “arrival time” colouring need no new run.
    • Engine. The time step runs in Rust compiled to WebAssembly; a JavaScript copy is the reference, and both give the same numbers.

    Check it: a 200 µm square window on (100), 60 min in 30 wt% KOH: simulated depth 47.32 µm (4 µm grid) and 47.56 µm (2 µm grid) against R(100)·t = 0.797 × 60 = 47.82 µm (Pal & Sato Table 4.1, p. 87). The {111} side walls stand at 54.73° with a 4 µm grid and 54.69° with a 2 µm grid, against 54.74°.

    10. How far to trust the numbers
    • Depths and undercuts are good to about one or two grid cells; corners are rounded over about two cells (numerical smoothing).
    • Silicon walls and beams thinner than about 8 cells, and windows narrower than about 5 cells, are not resolved well. Use the fine or very fine resolution, or wider features, when this matters. Example: the Corner lab default (KOH, H = 40 µm, no compensation on the left) gives a corner shift U/H of 1.47 at dx = 2.5 µm (normal) and 1.58 at dx = 1.75 µm (fine), a difference of about two cells over H; when such a number matters, compare two resolutions.
    • Rates between measured orientations, the use of one Q for planes without data, and the concentration interpolation are assumptions (see 3, 4 and 7). The convex-corner bevel in KOH comes out about 5° smaller than measured because the rate map has no {411} data (see 6).
    • Not modelled: hydrogen bubbles, stirring, surface roughness and hillocks, etch stops, mask etching except as a readout, and real KOH with additives (IPA, surfactants), which changes the anisotropy (Pal & Sato pp. 82, 112–117).
    11. Check yourself
    1. Why does a more concentrated KOH solution etch (100) more slowly?
      AnswerWater is a reactant in Si + 2H₂O + 2OH⁻ → Si(OH)₂O₂²⁻ + 2H₂; at higher KOH concentration there is less water (Pal & Sato p. 88).
    2. A square 300 µm opening on (100), edges along the flat. How deep is the pit when the {111} walls meet?
      Answer300/√2 = 212 µm (tan 54.74° = √2). In 30 wt% KOH at 70 °C that takes 212/0.797 ≈ 266 min. Try it in the Etched pit.
    3. You raise 40 wt% KOH from 70 °C to 90 °C. By how much does R(100) change, with Q = 0.60 eV?
      Answerexp[−(0.60/8.617×10⁻⁵)(1/363.15 − 1/343.15)] = 3.06, so 0.599 → 1.83 µm/min. Make it in the Custom etchant dialog and read the result table.
    4. Why is the convex corner of a mesa undercut but the concave corner of a pit is not?
      AnswerAt a concave corner the slowest planes ({111}) bound the shape; at a convex corner the fastest planes take over and etch under the mask (Lec 4-1 p. 8; Lec 10 p. 14). See the Corner lab.
